GenJi: The Digital Educator Who Built a Million-Strong Classroom on Bilibili

Dabao Zhao October 6, 2026

GenJi, whose real name is Li Genji, is a Chinese digital education creator and one of the most prominent figures in Bilibili's knowledge section. Operating under the account name "GenJi Really Wants to Teach You" (GenJi Shi Zhen Xiang Jiao Hui Ni), he has built a following of over ten million users on Bilibili, making him one of the platform's most-followed educational creators. His rise from a university student offering free lectures in borrowed classrooms to a nationally recognized UP creator is a story about the convergence of technical skill, pedagogical conviction, and the expanding reach of China's online knowledge economy. In January 2026, he was named to Bilibili's annual Top 100 UP Creators list, a distinction that confirmed his status not merely as a popular account but as a defining voice in the platform's educational ecosystem.

Li Genji's path into teaching began long before he ever opened a video editing program. As a primary school student, he was already the classmate others turned to for help with difficult problems, a role he describes as his first experience of explaining complex ideas to peers. That instinct followed him into university, where he developed a rare combination of design, programming, and product development skills. He became proficient in front-end and back-end development as well as visual interaction design, and by his early undergraduate years he was already serving as the youngest lecturer at his institution. It was during this period that he identified a disconnect that would shape the rest of his career: many university graduates struggled to find employment not because they lacked intelligence or effort, but because the skills taught in classrooms had little direct application to the demands of the professional world. The gap between what students were learning and what employers actually needed became the problem he set out to solve.

His response was to begin offering free public lectures across universities in central China, covering topics ranging from design tools to programming techniques. The lectures were practical by design, focused on skills that students could immediately apply rather than abstract theory. He continued this practice for nearly a decade, preparing course materials, coordinating venues, and teaching without any financial compensation. The work was demanding and often invisible, but the feedback he received from students who landed jobs or completed difficult graduation projects using what they had learned convinced him that the effort was worthwhile. He founded a student organization called Cloud Workshop (Yunding Zuofang), whose logo, a ladder stretching into the clouds, symbolized his belief that knowledge should function as a ladder for upward mobility. That logo remains on his Bilibili avatar today, a small but telling marker of continuity between his early volunteer teaching and his current platform career.

The transition from offline lectures to online video creation came in 2020, when the pandemic forced him to pause his in-person classes. Rather than abandon the work, he began recording the same practical lessons he had been delivering in person and uploading them to Bilibili. His first upload, a Photoshop tutorial recorded without elaborate production values or scripted performance, accumulated over 1.42 million views. The success was not the result of algorithmic luck but of a simple insight: learners wanted instruction that felt like a real classroom, not a polished entertainment product. GenJi's videos retain that classroom quality. They are structured, patient, and grounded in the assumption that the viewer is genuinely there to learn something useful. His speaking style is direct and unadorned, and his editing favors clarity over spectacle, a deliberate choice that distinguishes him from creators who prioritize production value over instructional substance.

As his audience grew, GenJi expanded his subject matter from design and programming into artificial intelligence, a shift that aligned both with his own technical interests and with the broader cultural moment. His AI-related content explains machine learning concepts, demonstrates practical applications of AI tools, and addresses the anxieties many learners feel about automation and job displacement. He approaches AI not as a distant technological marvel but as a set of tools that ordinary people can learn to use, a framing that has resonated with students, career-changers, and professionals seeking to remain relevant in a rapidly changing economy. His teaching philosophy, which he summarizes as "really wanting to teach you," is embedded in his account name and functions as both a promise to his audience and a standard against which he measures his own work. The phrase captures his rejection of performative education, the kind of content that gestures at learning without delivering it.

The scale of GenJi's platform presence reflects the demand for this kind of instruction. His account has surpassed ten million followers on Bilibili, placing him among the platform's most-subscribed individual creators. He has been featured in Chinese media, including a profile in Youth Daily that traced his evolution from a primary school helper to a digital education pioneer, and his selection for the 2025 Bilibili Top 100 UP Creators list placed him alongside figures like Luo Xiang and Lao Fanqie as one of the platform's defining creators. His influence extends beyond his own channel: he has built a broader educational ecosystem that includes self-developed intelligent hardware and a suite of learning resources designed to make technical skills accessible to users who may not have formal training in computer science. The Cloud Workshop logo on his avatar is a reminder that his project began as a voluntary effort to share knowledge, not as a commercial enterprise, and that the values informing his current work remain rooted in that origin.

The social value of GenJi's work lies in his demonstration that digital platforms can function as genuine educational infrastructure rather than merely as entertainment distribution networks. Bilibili's knowledge section emerged as a significant content category in the late 2010s and early 2020s, and creators like GenJi have been central to its legitimacy. By offering rigorous, practical instruction at no cost to anyone with an internet connection, he has contributed to a broader shift in how Chinese young people think about learning, career preparation, and the relationship between formal education and self-directed study. His emphasis on "knowledge universalization" (zhishi puhui) reflects a conviction that access to practical skills should not be determined by institutional affiliation or economic capacity. For viewers who cannot afford expensive training programs or who live far from major educational centers, his videos offer a pathway to competence that would otherwise be unavailable.

In summary, GenJi represents a model of the creator as educator, someone who treats the platform not as a stage for self-promotion but as a classroom that can accommodate millions of students simultaneously. His decade of unpaid public teaching before his online success is not merely a biographical detail but the foundation of his credibility: he earned his audience by helping people learn, not by chasing trends. His expansion into AI education reflects an awareness that the skills people need are changing, and his willingness to adapt his teaching to new technologies without abandoning his commitment to practical usefulness suggests that his influence will remain relevant as the educational landscape continues to evolve. For Bilibili, he is evidence that the platform's knowledge ambitions are not aspirational rhetoric but measurable reality. For learners, he is a reminder that the most valuable educational content is often the least flashy, and that a teacher who genuinely wants to teach is worth more than a thousand polished performances.

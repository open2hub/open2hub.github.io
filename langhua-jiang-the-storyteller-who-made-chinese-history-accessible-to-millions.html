Langhua Jiang: The Storyteller Who Made Chinese History Accessible to Millions

Dabao Zhao October 6, 2026

Langhua Jiang, whose real name is Jiang Huajie, is a Chinese history content creator and one of the most distinctive voices in Bilibili's knowledge ecosystem. Born in 1997 in Rizhao, Shandong Province, and currently based in Nanjing, he is a member of the generation that Chinese media often refers to as the "post-95s," a cohort that came of age alongside the rise of China's digital platforms. His channel, "Langhua Jiang," which translates roughly to "Waves and Ginger," has accumulated over three million followers across Bilibili and Douyin, with total video views exceeding one hundred million. His self-positioning is deliberately modest: he describes himself not as a historian but as a "storyteller of Chinese history," a phrase that captures both the narrative ambition of his work and his refusal to claim academic authority he does not possess. In less than three years, he has built a body of work that includes series on ancient Chinese daily life, folklore and江湖 culture, and museum artifacts, including a single video lasting four hours and thirteen minutes that surveys all 195 Chinese cultural relics prohibited from overseas exhibition.

Jiang Huajie's path to content creation was neither direct nor instantaneous. Before launching his Bilibili channel in June 2023, he had experimented with WeChat public accounts, podcasts, and vlogs, all without finding a stable audience or a clear creative identity. His breakthrough came with his very first Bilibili upload, a video titled "This Might Be the Best History UP Creator Recommendation on Bilibili," in which he simply recommended other history creators he admired. The video climbed to Bilibili's trending page the following day and quickly surpassed one million views, a result he later described as a "three-way win" in which the recommended creators gained attention, the audience received useful recommendations, and he himself discovered what resonated with viewers. That experience taught him a lesson that would shape his subsequent work: effective communication requires a "利他性," an orientation toward the audience's needs rather than the creator's self-expression. He subsequently produced six or seven more videos before settling on the creative direction that now defines his channel, a gradual process of refinement rather than a single moment of clarity.

What distinguishes Langhua Jiang from the crowded field of Chinese history content creators is his ability to make obscure, often overlooked aspects of the past feel immediately relevant and genuinely fascinating. His videos are organized around several recurring series. The "Chinese Encyclopedia History" series functions as a comprehensive guide to the daily lives of ancient Chinese people, covering topics ranging from clothing and food to the imperial examination system and funeral customs. The "Jianghu History" series explores the folk traditions and underworld stories of bandits, tomb raiders, and con artists that rarely appear in official historical narratives. The "Understanding Museums" series focuses on the artifacts and institutions that preserve China's material heritage. His content is characterized by a relaxed, conversational delivery that avoids academic jargon while still respecting the complexity of his subjects. He has described his approach as "thinning out" history, a metaphor that captures his method of extracting the most engaging and accessible elements from dense historical material without sacrificing accuracy. His videos are long by contemporary standards, often running twenty to thirty minutes, and he has explicitly rejected the pressure to conform to short-video conventions, a decision that has allowed him to build a dedicated audience willing to invest time in substantive content.

Among his most significant works is the 253-minute video "A Complete Tour of China's Top National Treasures in One Episode," released on June 1, 2025. The video surveys all 195 Chinese cultural relics that are prohibited from being taken abroad for exhibition, organized geographically and presented with the same accessible storytelling style that characterizes his shorter content. The production took nearly a year to complete, a period during which he maintained his regular weekly upload schedule simultaneously. The video was selected for Bilibili's "Weekly Must-Watch" list and has accumulated over 2.5 million views, an extraordinary figure for a four-hour history documentary. In an interview, Jiang explained that the project was a long-held personal ambition: he wanted viewers to be able to walk into any museum in China and encounter "familiar faces," artifacts whose stories they already knew. The video's success demonstrates that there is a substantial audience for deep, long-form historical content, even in an era dominated by short-form video. Other notable works include his video "How Did Ancient Chinese People Survive the Coldest Winters?" which accumulated over 2.5 million views within ten days of its release, and his "Chinese Surnames" series, which traces the origins of Chinese family names and has been widely shared among viewers interested in their own ancestral history.

Langhua Jiang's platform achievements reflect the growing prominence of knowledge content on Bilibili. His Bilibili follower count surpassed one million in June 2025, coinciding with the release of his national treasures video, and he has since grown to over three million followers across platforms. His "Chinese Encyclopedia History" series alone has accumulated 120 million views, while other series such as "Chinese Culinary History" and "Jianghu Strange Tales" have each reached tens of millions of views. In 2025, he published a book titled "An Illustrated Guide to Ancient Life and Culture," extending his work from video into print media. He has been recognized with Douyin's Annual Hardcore Content Award, and his videos have been cited in media outlets including The Paper and Southern Metropolis Daily as examples of the "new mass literature and art" movement, a cultural trend in which grassroots creators use digital platforms to make specialized knowledge accessible to general audiences.

The broader social value of Langhua Jiang's work lies in his demonstration that historical knowledge does not need to be simplified or sensationalized to reach a mass audience. He approaches history from the perspective of an ordinary person rather than an academic specialist, and he has argued that this outsider position is precisely what allows him to identify which aspects of the past will resonate with viewers who have no formal training in history. His videos encourage viewers to see history not as a collection of dates and names to be memorized but as a living resource for understanding human behavior, social organization, and cultural continuity. For young Chinese viewers who may feel disconnected from traditional culture, his work offers an accessible entry point that respects their intelligence while meeting them where they are. His emphasis on the "usefulness" of historical knowledge, not in a utilitarian sense but in the sense of enriching one's understanding of the present, aligns with Bilibili's broader mission to position itself as a platform where entertainment and education coexist.

In summary, Langhua Jiang represents a model of the creator as public storyteller, someone who uses digital media to bridge the gap between specialized knowledge and general curiosity. His decision to prioritize long-form, deeply researched content over algorithm-friendly short videos is a calculated risk that has paid off, both commercially and creatively. His background as a self-described "layperson" rather than a trained historian gives his work a relatability that more authoritative voices sometimes lack, while his commitment to accuracy and thorough research ensures that accessibility does not come at the expense of substance. For Bilibili, he is evidence that the platform's knowledge section can support creators who take intellectual risks and expect their audiences to follow. For the broader landscape of Chinese digital culture, he is a reminder that the most enduring content is often that which helps people understand themselves and their world a little better, one story at a time.
